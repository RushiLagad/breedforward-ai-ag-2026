When the plots are cut, sample every family and let the families speak

A plot-cut decision in a maize breeding program, worked end to end on a million historical testcross plots and scored against the real yields of lines the models never saw.

Sambhavi PatelRushikesh LagadRenuka KhanalAjaydeep BediGrounded in data. Breeding for the future.

The decision

January 2008. The plots are cut. Which lines go in the ground?

A maize breeding program is about to plant its 2008 season. It has 15,968 new inbred lines from 998 biparental families, all genotyped, ready to be crossed to testers and put in the field. Behind them sit seven years of testcross data: 2001 to 2007, about a million plots across 1,185 year-by-location environments, with 84 weather and soil covariates per environment and one shared 2,911-SNP marker panel.

Then the field budget is cut. There are no longer enough plots to grow every candidate. Bayer's question to us was in two parts, and they are answered at two different times.

After the sampled plots are harvestedWhich of the lines that were never planted should advancethe above, plus the phenotypes of the siblings that were planted

The genotype-only results below are a January decision. The sibling results are not: they assume half of every family was planted and harvested, and they rank the half that was never planted. The only thing those models never see is the real 2008 yield of the untested lines, which exists in the data because in reality every line was grown. It is used purely to score the decision. We say this early because it is easy to hear "predict 2008" and assume everything is a preseason forecast.

We treated this as a decision problem, not a prediction contest. The scenario specifies broad-acre or environment-specific prediction as a choice to justify, general combining ability as the target (specific combining ability is out of scope by the organizers' statement), and a real 2008 season to be judged against.

The data

What a million plots of legacy testcross data actually contain

The dataset is Bayer Crop Science's legacy maize testcross data, published as Lian, Jacobson, Zhong and Bernardo (2014, Crop Science 54:1514), provided in simplified form by the organizers. Two heterotic groups, C1 and C2, each a set of biparental populations: two inbred parents crossed, their progeny inbred, then each progeny line crossed to a tester and grown in yield trials. Every record is one plot: a line, its tester, a year, a location, and eight traits.

Traits

Grain yield (YLD, bu/ac), grain moisture at harvest (MST), test weight (TWT), relative maturity (ERM), root lodging (RTLP), stalk lodging (STLP), plant height (PHT) and ear height (EHT). Yield is the trait the decision is about; moisture, test weight and maturity are the traits a breeder will not ignore for it; lodging is the trait that removes a line no matter how it yields.

Structure that shapes every method choice

  • Every line is tested in exactly one year. There is no such thing as a line's performance in a previous season. Whatever we know about a 2008 line before 2008 comes from its genotype, its parents, and its siblings.
  • Families are new each year. 71% of the 2008 families have neither parent seen in any earlier year; only 29% have both. Pedigree connectedness to the past is thin, and it turns out to be the single number that best predicts how well any genotype-only model will do.
  • Each line mean rests on about five plots. Line-mean heritability of yield is 0.48 at about seven plots and the 2008 line means have reliability 0.46 at 4.8 plots. That sets a ceiling: no predictor can correlate with the observed 2008 line means above about 0.68, because the line means themselves are that noisy.
  • One marker panel for everyone. 2,911 SNPs, imputed, shared across both groups. Within a biparental family the markers segregate; across families they also carry the parents' identity.
  • Environments are described richly. 84 covariates per year-location: monthly precipitation, cooling degree days, temperature, soil texture, organic carbon, pH. Enough to type environments by stress regime and ask whether lines re-rank between them.

The traps we measured before modelling

  • LINE is unique only within a family; the same number appears in hundreds of families. Every join is keyed on LINE_UNIQUE_ID. Missing this silently merges unrelated lines.
  • The C2 phenotype file stores the line number as 12.0 and the unique id as C2.1.12.0; the genotype files use 12. Strip the .0, then the leading zeros, or every C2 genotype fails to match and the marker model silently trains on half the data.
  • 52,412 C2 plots have no matching environment record and are dropped by the environment join; 3.9% of plots have no tester id and are carried under a single "unknown tester" level.
  • 15,968 lines appear in the raw 2008 files, 15,967 survive the environment join, 15,959 have a scorable 2008 yield. Three counts, all correct, each for a different question.
  • The data guide's column names do not match the files (YEAR_x, X04_PRCP, clay_0_5cm; no TMAX or TMIN). The build script uses the real names and asserts on them.

After the build: 1,019,864 plots, 154,551 lines, 998 families, 1,185 environments in one table, with each environment's stress regime attached.

The hypothesis

Stated so that it could be wrong

What we set out to test
In a new year with mostly new families, the genotype alone says little about a line's yield. Most of what can be known about a line lives in its family. A program facing a plot cut should therefore phenotype a small sample of every family instead of every line of a few families, predict the untested siblings from the family mean, and use markers only where they add on top of that.

Two results would have falsified it. If a marker model trained on 2001 to 2007 had predicted 2008 yield well on its own, the right answer would have been to skip the field sampling and trust the genotypes. If lines had re-ranked across environment types, a broad-acre family mean would have been the wrong quantity to predict, and the program would need environment-specific testing. We tested both before building the recommendation, and the recommendation depends on both tests coming out the way they did.

The framing is ours; the mathematics is established. Ridge regression on markers is the GBLUP equivalent, family means are the oldest estimator in plant breeding, and the sampling curve is a resource-allocation experiment. We chose established mathematics deliberately: a weekend is long enough to ask a sharp question of a large dataset, not to validate a new estimator.

Methodology

Seven stages, each with a baseline and a ceiling

Every stage is one script, run in order, and every number below was reproduced on a second machine with identical output. The rule throughout: the 2008 season is never touched until a model is frozen, and every accuracy is reported next to what a naive baseline gets and what perfect information would get.

Build one clean table

Read both groups with the id fixes above, attach the environment table by year and location, and type each environment by a flowering-window stress index: z(July plus August precipitation) minus z(July plus August cooling degree days), cut into tertiles over unique environments (HotDry, Moderate, CoolWet).

Why tertiles on unique environments rather than on plots: a handful of large trials would otherwise define the regimes. Why July and August: silking and grain fill are when maize yield is set.

Centre yield within environment and tester

Every trait is expressed as the deviation from the mean of its environment × tester cell. This removes location and year effects and every tester's own contribution in one step, so what remains is the line's general combining ability against that tester's background. A line's value is the mean of its centred plots. In the sibling stages the cell means are computed from training plots only (every pre-2008 plot plus the phenotyped 2008 siblings) and then applied to all plots, so the untested lines' outcomes never touch the target definition.

This is also why specific combining ability never enters: tester effects are removed before any model sees the data, consistent with the scenario's GCA-only statement.

Test for G × E before choosing a target

Compute each line's value separately in HotDry and CoolWet environments and correlate them across lines (the "between" correlation). Then split the plots within a single regime in half at random and correlate the two halves (the "within" reliability). If lines truly re-rank between regimes, between will sit well below within. If between equals within, the apparent re-ranking is noise.

This is the honest version of the question. A low between-regime correlation on its own proves nothing, because the within-regime reliability is also low at five plots per line. Repeated at the parent level (parents with 30 or more environments) as a higher-power check.

Genotype-only prediction, two hold-out years

Ridge regression of centred line values on the 2,911 markers, fit by accumulating XTX in blocks (the GBLUP-equivalent for this marker count). The shrinkage parameter is chosen on a 2007 hold-out (train 2000 to 2006) from a grid that corresponds to plot-level heritabilities of 0.05 to 0.7, then frozen. Only then is 2008 predicted (train 2000 to 2007). Six traits. Baselines: the family mean from earlier years where the family was seen before, and parent GCA from earlier years. Ceiling: the reliability of the 2008 line means.

This is the CV00 scheme of Burgueño et al. (2012): new lines in a new year. It is the scheme the scenario describes, and the one the literature reports lowest accuracies for.

Sibling-informed prediction

The scenario says phenotypes of "some related lines" are available. We simulate it: a stratified half of every 2008 family is declared planted and phenotyped (at least one line planted and one held back per family, 7,987 lines, 38,713 of the season's 77,353 plots); the other 7,972 lines are predicted and scored. Four schemes on the same untested lines. A: genotype only, trained on 2001 to 2007. B: genotype, with the phenotyped 2008 siblings pooled into training. C: the mean of the phenotyped siblings, no markers. D: the sibling mean plus a within-family ridge on marker deviations from that mean (the Mendelian sampling term), fit for families with 30 or more phenotyped siblings.

D is the two-stage model: the family carries the between-family variance, the markers are asked only to rank siblings within it. B is what a naive "add the new data to the training set" approach does, and it turns out to be the wrong way to use the same information.

The sampling curve

Repeat stage 5 with the phenotyped share of every family set to 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 60% and 75%, stratified so every family gets at least one planted line, three random draws each, family mean with and without the within-family marker term, scored on real 2008 yield. The x-axis is the actual plots of the sampled lines; the y-axis is realised gain if the top 20% advance. This is the January question answered directly.

The advancement index and its sensitivity

For each untested line, the stage 5 scheme D prediction for yield, moisture, test weight and maturity, plus the observed lodging of its phenotyped siblings (lodging is not predictable from markers here, so it enters as a family penalty). Index = 0.5·z(yield) + 0.1·z(test weight) − 0.15·z(moisture) − 0.05·z(maturity) − 0.2·z(family lodging), ties broken on line id. The top 20% by index is the advancement set, scored by its realised 2008 yield. Both yield predictors (C and D) are scored on the full index and the choice is recorded. Then seven named alternative weightings and 500 random ones within plausible ranges, to see how much the set and its outcome move.

The weights are a breeder's judgment, not an estimate; the sensitivity analysis is how we make that judgment inspectable instead of hidden. The demo exposes the same weights as sliders.

The one trade-off we made, stated. The advancement list uses scheme D for yield even though C wins yield alone. Scored on the full index, the two are tied (+4.15 against +4.13 bu/ac realised). But a family mean gives every sibling the same yield prediction, so the index can only rank families: the flagged set collapses onto 52 families. Two-stage ranks within families and keeps 115. Our rule, written into the code and the summary table: use two-stage when the index gains are within 0.1 bu/ac, otherwise the higher. That is a judgment about family coverage and within-family choice, worth about 0.02 bu/ac, and the alternative's outcome is recorded next to it.

Interpretation

Why the family beats the genome here, and when it would not

The between-family variance is where the money is. In a biparental breeding scheme, the differences between families (which two parents were crossed) are large and the differences within a family (Mendelian sampling) are, per line, buried under five plots of field noise. A family mean estimated from a few phenotyped siblings averages that noise down and captures the between-family component directly. The marker model has to learn the same between-family component from the parents' marker identity, which it can only do for parents it has seen before, and 71% of 2008 families have no parent in the training data.

Pooling siblings into training is the wrong way to use them. Scheme B gives the ridge model the same phenotyped siblings, and it reaches only r 0.24. Under heavy shrinkage (which the 2007 tuning demanded), a few hundred new lines from a family cannot pull 2,911 coefficients far from what a million historical plots set them to. The family mean uses the same plots with no shrinkage at all, because it estimates one number per family, not 2,911. The two-stage model separates the jobs: family mean for between, markers for within.

Markers earn their keep on the grain-quality traits. Moisture and test weight have higher heritability at the plot level, so the within-family marker term has signal to find. Yield does not, at five plots per line. This is consistent with the by-trait pattern in the genotype-only stage (test weight 0.23, moisture 0.18, yield 0.14).

The G × E result is a statement about resolution, not about biology. A two-month weather index cut into tertiles is coarse. Lines certainly interact with environments; what we found is that at this resolution and this plot count, the interaction does not change rankings more than noise does, so predicting a broad-acre value loses nothing measurable. A finer environmental typing, or a program that tests in genuinely contrasting target populations of environments, could find rank-changing interaction that this test cannot see.

The ceiling matters for how to read every number. An r of 0.36 against line means with reliability 0.46 corresponds to an accuracy of about 0.5 against true genetic value. Through the breeder's equation with selection intensity 1.40 for the top 20% and an additive standard deviation of about 6.45 bu/ac, r 0.14 buys +1.3 bu/ac per cycle, r 0.35 buys +3.2 and r 0.50 buys +4.5. The realised gains we observe (+1.9 and +4.1) sit where that arithmetic says they should.

When this would not hold. In a program with high connectedness (recycled parents, dense pedigrees), the genotype-only model would do much better and the sampling curve would start higher. In a program with far more plots per line, the within-family marker term would matter for yield too. In a program whose target environments truly split the germplasm, environment-specific prediction would win. Each of those is a measurable condition, and the diagnostics in this pipeline are how a program would check which world it is in.

For the breeding program

How a commercial program would put this to work

Everything below is a policy the pipeline supports with a number it already computes, written the way a breeding operations team would need it. None of it requires a new estimator; it requires running the diagnostics every cycle and letting them set the field plan.

Field plan

Allocate plots per family, not per line, and never drop a family

In a plot-cut year, the first rule is coverage: every family gets a minimum sample. The sampling curve is the budgeting tool. Read the plot budget off the x-axis and the expected gain off the y-axis; at this program's scale, 10% of every family (7,774 plots) realises 92% of what half of every family (38,726 plots) realises. The marginal plot after 10% coverage buys almost nothing in ranking accuracy and should be spent on replication of the shortlist instead. Stratify the sample: at least one line per family, always.

Evidence: Figure 3. Families dropped from the sample can only be ranked by the genotype-only model, at r 0.13, which is close to a coin toss for advancement; sampling them independently instead of by family cost 0.6 bu/ac at the 10% point in our own first version.

Prediction

Predict untested siblings from the family mean plus a within-family marker model

Two-stage, in this order: the family mean of the phenotyped siblings carries the between-family value; a within-family ridge on marker deviations ranks siblings inside it, fit only where the family has enough phenotyped members (30 in our run). Use the global marker model only for a family with zero plots. Do not pool the new season's siblings into the global training set and expect the model to learn from them; it does not, under the shrinkage a new-year model needs.

Evidence: scheme C beats scheme B (0.36 vs 0.24) on identical information; scheme D matches C on the full index (+4.13 vs +4.15) and keeps 115 families in the advancement set instead of 52.

Markers

Spend marker effort where it pays: grain quality and zero-plot families

Within-family markers lift moisture (0.59 to 0.64), test weight (0.50 to 0.54) and maturity (0.38 to 0.50) and do nothing for yield at this plot count. Use them to make the dry-down and test-weight calls on the untested siblings, to rank siblings within a family for advancement, and as the only handle on families that got no plots. Do not present genotype-only yield prediction across unconnected new families as a substitute for field sampling; it is not, here.

Evidence: by-trait accuracies in stages 4 and 5; the marker term's contribution on the sampling curve.

Target

Predict broad-acre by default, and re-test that choice every cycle

The between-versus-within diagnostic is cheap and should run each year on the new season's data: line values in two contrasting environment types, correlated across lines, against a split-half reliability within one type. While between equals within, predict a single broad-acre value and use the environment covariates to characterise trials, not to split the germplasm. If between ever drops well below within, that is the signal to move to environment-specific prediction for that trait.

Evidence: line-level 0.23 vs 0.18 to 0.25; parent-level 0.50 vs 0.45 to 0.64; regime explains 1% of environment mean yield.

Selection

Advance on an explicit index, and publish its sensitivity

Write the weights down, run the named alternatives and a random sweep every cycle, and report overlap and realised outcome the way Figure 4 does. The weights remain the breeder's call; what changes is that the call is inspectable. The one rule the sweep gives: never let secondary traits carry as much weight as yield, or the index selects for earliness and dryness at the cost of most of the gain. Lodging enters as an observed family penalty, not a prediction, because it is not predictable from markers at this scale.

Evidence: 87% median overlap across 500 weightings; equal weights keep only a third of the gain.

Leading indicator

Track pedigree connectedness as a forecast of prediction accuracy

Before the season, compute the share of new families whose parents appear in the training data. It doubled from 15% to 29% between 2007 and 2008 and the genotype-only accuracy doubled with it. A program can steer this: recycling proven parents, or including a connected check set in each new family, raises the accuracy of every marker-based call in the following year. It is also the honest way to set expectations for how much a genomic model can deliver in a given cycle.

Evidence: 2007 r 0.07 at 15% connectedness; 2008 r 0.14 at 29%.

Data

Fix the identifiers and the joins before the modelling, every time

Three of the traps we measured would each silently corrupt a genomic prediction: a within-family line number treated as a global id, a formatting mismatch between phenotype and genotype ids, and environment records missing for 5% of plots. A production pipeline should assert on these: globally unique line ids, a genotype match rate reported per family, tester ids present, environment coverage reported per trial, and a column-name contract between the data guide and the files.

Evidence: the C2 match failure and the 52,412 dropped plots, both found and measured in stage 1.

Cadence

Run it as an annual cycle with a backtest and a post-harvest audit

January: build the table, run the connectedness and G × E diagnostics, freeze the model on the previous year's hold-out, plant a stratified sample of every family per the curve. After the sampled plots are harvested: predict the untested siblings from the sample, apply the index, produce the ranked list and the sensitivity report. Next season: score the realised outcome of the advanced set against the candidates, exactly as this study scored 2008, and feed the result into next year's expectations. The pipeline already does all three; the only change is that the "real 2008" becomes each new season in turn.

Value

What the plots buy, in the program's own units

2008 as run used 77,353 plots on 15,959 lines. Sampling half of every family used 38,713 plots and realised +4.1 bu/ac on the untested half under the index; sampling 10% used 7,774 plots and realised +4.5 by family mean alone. Multiply plots saved by the program's cost per plot; we do not know that number and have not guessed it. On the gain side, the breeder's equation gives the expected yield advance per cycle from accuracy: +1.3 bu/ac at r 0.14, +3.2 at r 0.35, +4.5 at r 0.50, with an additive standard deviation of about 6.45 bu/ac and 20% selected.

Extensions

What two more weeks would add, in order

Replace the ridge with a GBLUP that carries a pedigree-augmented relationship matrix, so connected parents contribute explicitly. Replace the random half-split with a designed sparse-testing layout (partially replicated, families balanced across locations) and re-run the curve. Fit a factor-analytic multi-environment model on the 84 covariates as a stronger G × E test. Add a multi-trait model so moisture and test weight information sharpens the yield prediction. Extend validation to 2006 and 2007 for the sibling schemes, not just 2008.

Limits

What we would not claim

  • One year of validation for the sibling schemes (2008), two for genotype-only (2007, 2008). The sampling curve's shape is one season's evidence.
  • Random half-splits within families. A breeder chooses which siblings to plant, and a designed sample would likely do better than our random one.
  • Ridge treats every SNP the same. No trait-specific genetic architecture, no dominance, no pedigree beyond what the markers carry.
  • Lodging traits are near-unpredictable here and enter only as an observed family mean. A program with lodging pressure needs a different handle on them.
  • The stress regimes are a two-month weather index in tertiles. "No rank-changing G × E at this resolution" is the claim; "no G × E" is not.
  • The realised gains are top-20% comparisons on line means with reliability 0.46. They are conservative relative to true genetic gain and should be read next to the ceiling.
  • The sibling stages are an after-harvest decision, not a preseason forecast. The preseason answer is the sampling curve; the genotype-only model is the only preseason ranking.
  • Two judgments are in the pipeline and both are recorded with the alternative's outcome: the index weights, and two-stage over the sibling mean for yield (worth 0.02 bu/ac, bought 63 more families in the flagged set).
  • The Windows reproduction matched to within rounding, not bit for bit: one stage 5 cell differed in the third decimal. Versions are pinned in requirements-lock.txt and inputs are identified by SHA-256.

Reproduce it

One repository, eight scripts, every number
